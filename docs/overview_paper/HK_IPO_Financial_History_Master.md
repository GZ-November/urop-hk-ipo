# Hong Kong’s IPO Market: A Financial History of the City, China, and Global Capital, 1891–2026

*Comprehensive Research Monograph — Historical Coverage: 1891–2026; Reconciled IPO Census: January 1994–September 2026; Market Observation Cutoff: November 12, 2026*

**Abstract.** How does an offshore port city turn incompatible legal, monetary, and political systems into a functioning equity market, and who shares the resulting wealth, institutional change, and costs? This monograph examines 135 years of equity issuance in Hong Kong (1891–2026), pairing institutional history with a census, built from HKEX records, Bloomberg data, and offering documents, of all 2,866 ordinary operating-company initial public offerings (HK\$5.66 trillion in gross proceeds) completed between January 1994 and September 2026. While annual U.S. IPO counts fell by roughly three-quarters after 2000, Hong Kong's listing pace nearly doubled. It did so by combining Common Law governance, a U.S.-dollar currency board, sponsor diligence, and cornerstone commitments to turn Mainland growth into securities global investors could price.

That record brought turbulence and failure alongside extraordinary wealth creation. Measured from the first-day close, the typical offering trailed passive benchmarks over its first year, and about three-quarters of verified issuers traded below their offer price by November 2026. Net price gains on the offered shares nevertheless reached about HK\$3.7 trillion before dividends, because a tiny group of winners, led by Tencent and AIA, generated nearly three-quarters of all positive gains. Those gains comfortably exceed a price-only Hang Seng investment of the same dollars but fall short of one with dividends reinvested. An IPO in Hong Kong is also rarely a one-off cash raise. Post-IPO equity issuance often exceeds IPO proceeds, recurring market income dwarfs listing fees, and about half of direct going-public costs pay for non-underwriting professional and regulatory services. Mainland enterprises, in turn, remade a local property-and-bank bourse into a global China-asset hub, with Mainland banks now on roughly nine in ten sponsor teams.

## Section 1. Introduction: Capital Formation, Institutional Bridges, and Distributional Trade-offs

### 1.1 What an IPO Market Is For—and Two Divergent Global Paths

At its core, finance exists to move purchasing power from savers with surplus capital to enterprises that can deploy it productively—pricing uncertain future cash flows, enforcing claims across time, and compensating investors for bearing risk (Levine, 1997, 2005; Rajan and Zingales, 1998). In modern corporate finance, the initial public offering (IPO) sits at the hinge of that allocation process: going public converts illiquid founder and venture stakes into tradable shares, supplies permanent equity for expansion, establishes an external price signal, and subjects corporate insiders to continuous public scrutiny (Ritter and Welch, 2002; Ljungqvist, 2007; Lowry, Michaely, and Volkova, 2017). Yet in the world’s largest capital market, the public corporation has spent the past quarter-century in steady retreat. As documented by Gao, Ritter, and Zhu (2013), Lowry, Michaely, and Volkova (2017), and Doidge, Karolyi, and Stulz (2017), annual U.S. operating-company IPO volume fell by roughly three-quarters after 2000—from an average of 451 listings per year in 1994–2000 to 113 per year across 2001–2025—as deep private venture pools, rising compliance burdens, and strategic acquisitions kept growing firms private.

Viewed from Hong Kong, the modern history of going public moved in the opposite direction. Between January 1994 and September 2026, Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing Limited (HKEX) and its predecessor exchanges admitted 2,866 ordinary operating-company IPOs—2,289 on the Main Board and 577 on the Growth Enterprise Market (GEM)—raising HK\$5,659.4 billion in gross primary-market proceeds. The comparison is indicative because the U.S. series applies Ritter's stricter operating-company screens, while the Hong Kong count includes small GEM offerings. Even so, far from shrinking after the turn of the century, combined Hong Kong issuance nearly doubled its annual pace from 50 listings per year during 1994–2000 to 96 per year across 2001–2025 (with 113 Main Board deals in 2025 and another 113 in the first nine months of 2026), repeatedly ranking first globally in annual IPO funds raised during the late 2000s, mid-2010s, and mid-2020s (Figure 1.1). For an offshore port city of roughly seven and a half million residents whose domestic factories had largely migrated across the Shenzhen River by the early 1990s, intermediating three decades of large-scale equity formation stands among the most consequential institutional experiments in modern financial history.

Why did this offshore market matter so profoundly? Because moving money to those who need it requires **cross-system trust production**. After 1993, Mainland China possessed fast-growing enterprises hungry for capital and modern governance, yet operated behind capital-account controls with a non-convertible currency and nascent corporate law; global institutional investors held abundant low-cost capital, yet were unwilling to assume unpriced legal and repatriation risks onshore. Hong Kong’s fundamental financial achievement was to assemble an institutional package—a U.S.-dollar currency board, Common Law corporate charters, international accounting audits, statutory sponsor liability, and pre-committed cornerstone lockups—that transformed otherwise uninvestable cross-border growth into standardized, priceable, and tradable equity. Across share-verified cohorts, more than four-fifths of offerings sold pure primary shares (83.3% overall, rising to 97.4% on the Main Board in 2023–2026), channeling roughly 94% of gross dollar proceeds directly into corporate balance sheets rather than insider cash-outs.

At the same time, Hong Kong illustrates a truth often overlooked in corporate finance textbooks: **an IPO is not a one-off fundraising transaction, but the starting line of a continuous public-capital ecosystem—and a force that reshapes both issuer and host market in tandem**. For **enterprises**, going public was the entry ticket to decades of seasoned follow-on equity placements, rights issues (which routinely exceeded annual IPO proceeds), and acquisition currency that funded bank recapitalizations, digital ecosystems, clinical drug pipelines, and overseas factories. For **global and local investors**, it delivered an audited Common Law gateway to Asian growth and, through landmark public-asset listings, a vehicle for Hong Kong household wealth. For **Hong Kong itself**, primary issuance anchored a self-reinforcing service economy in which about half of direct going-public expenses paid for non-underwriting professional and regulatory services, many of them based in the city, and recurring exchange trading and clearing revenues outstripped listing fees more than tenfold. Equally important, **this transformation was bidirectional**: Mainland issuers did not simply borrow Hong Kong’s infrastructure—they completely remade Hong Kong’s market, turning a colonial exchange once dominated by local property developers and British trading *hongs* into the world’s premier hub for Chinese assets, even as that success generated new structural tensions around Mainland sponsor concentration (a Mainland bank on roughly nine in ten recent deals), rising Southbound pricing influence, and the secondary-market marginalization of non-Mainland international issuers.

Nor was this thirty-year arc a frictionless ascent. Every major institutional leap in Hong Kong emerged out of crisis, excess, or structural failure—from the 1973–1974 crash and the four-day exchange closure of October 1987 that forced statutory regulation and paperless settlement, through the Asian and Global Financial Crises, GEM’s post-dot-com decline into illiquid shells, sponsor and accounting enforcement scandals, the loss of Alibaba’s 2014 IPO that compelled dual-class reform, and post-2020 regulatory halts and geopolitical rewiring.

Pairing aggregate fundraising with realized investor outcomes brings the market’s central financial tension into focus. *Do Hong Kong IPOs create wealth for investors, or destroy it?* As in Lowry, Michaely, and Volkova’s (2017) synthesis of U.S. post-IPO returns, the answer depends sharply on **who buys, at what price, and with what portfolio weights**. Evaluated from Day-1 closing prices ($P_1$), the typical offering trailed dividend-reinvested benchmarks and size-matched peers over its first year, and roughly three-quarters (77.5%) of 2,117 verified issuers traded below their nominal offer price by November 2026 (with GEM micro-caps faring far worse). Measured across all offered shares, however, cumulative net price appreciation reached **+HK\$3.71 trillion** before cash dividends.

Three structural mechanisms resolve this apparent paradox—and explain why global capital kept returning across thirty-three years:
1. **Primary Allocation ($P_0$) vs. Secondary Chasing ($P_1$)**: Investors who receive allocations buy at the offer price ($P_0$), buffered by more than HK\$613 billion in cumulative first-day underpricing (+11.8% proceeds-weighted). An investor who could have bought every Main Board deal at $P_0$ would have earned positive one-year benchmark-adjusted returns (+4.9% equal-weighted and +9.3% proceeds-weighted against the Hang Seng Total Return Index), whereas buyers at the first-day close would have trailed the benchmark. Actual institutional allocations are not observed.
2. **Deal-Size Weighting vs. Equal Weighting**: Value-weighted Main Board portfolios earn multi-year factor alphas close to zero, while negative equal-weighted averages are heavily driven by thinly traded micro-caps and stagnant GEM listings.
3. **The Cost of Openness—Right-Tail Skewness Meets Market Discipline**: Public markets are not omniscient screening machines; high tolerance at the gate and harsh clearing in the aftermarket are two sides of the same coin. Because no regulator or underwriter can identify future winners with certainty ex ante, keeping the listing window open inevitably admits firms that later disappoint—and Hong Kong’s secondary market disciplines those failures ruthlessly through valuation compression and vanishing liquidity. What sustains the aggregate bargain is venture-style right-tail skewness: the **top 1% of issuers (21 companies, led by Tencent, AIA, and major commercial banks) generated nearly three-quarters (73.8%) of all positive offered-tranche gains**, and the top 5% generated over 94%. Understanding how an offshore city sustained this high-variance equilibrium requires stepping back from isolated deal mechanics to view the entire financial system as one connected story.

### 1.2 A Complete Financial History: Core Questions and Intellectual Positioning

Existing academic literature on Hong Kong’s primary equity market has typically studied individual pieces of market microstructure in isolation—pre-1990s underpricing (McGuinness, 1992), retail subscription clawbacks (Agarwal, Liu, and Rhee, 2008), pre-IPO earnings management around listing thresholds (Chen, Lee, and Li, 2008), cornerstone allocations (McGuinness, 2014), or post-1993 state-enterprise privatizations (Sun and Tong, 2003; Jiang, Yue, and Zhao, 2009). Meanwhile, foundational frameworks in financial development (Levine, 1997, 2005; Wurgler, 2000), law and finance (La Porta et al., 1998; Allen, Qian, and Qian, 2005), cross-border bonding (Coffee, 1999, 2002; Stulz, 1999; Karolyi, 2006), and comparative IPO regulation (Lowry, Michaely, and Volkova, 2017; Qian, Ritter, and Shao, 2024) offer powerful lenses that have rarely been brought together across the full history of Asia’s premier cross-border equity hub.

This monograph integrates those traditions around four connected questions:

1. **Institutional Origins and Trust Production**: *How did Hong Kong make cross-border equity contracting believable across incompatible monetary, legal, and corporate systems?* Why did this market take root in Hong Kong rather than onshore in Shanghai or Shenzhen, offshore in Singapore or London, or solely in New York—and how did a century of exchange rivalries, speculative crashes, and clearing crises (1891–1993) forge the monetary anchor, Common Law wrappers, and digital plumbing that made the 1993 H-share breakthrough possible?
2. **Market Mechanisms and Incentive Design**: *How do pricing, gatekeeping, retail allocation, margin financing, and aftermarket trading divide risk and rents in practice?* When hard price ceilings cap bookbuilding, mandatory clawbacks reallocate shares between institutions and leveraged retail buyers, cornerstone investors lock up a third to half of the deal upfront, and stabilization managers defend debut prices, who captures primary rents and how quickly do secondary prices discipline fundamentals?
3. **Real-Economy and Urban Transformation**: *What did this market actually build—for Hong Kong, for China’s corporate sector, and for global capital?* How much professional-service income, recurring exchange business, and household wealth stayed inside the city, and how did IPO and follow-on capital translate into bank recapitalizations, digital platforms, clinical drug pipelines, and global factories?
4. **Failures, Trade-offs, and Counterfactuals**: *Who absorbed the losses and compliance costs, why did non-Mainland multinationals struggle to retain secondary liquidity, and what would have happened without Hong Kong?* As Mainland underwriting share and Southbound trading grow alongside persistent international asset-management pools, what structural trade-offs now govern Hong Kong’s future between domestic small-cap access and global cross-border intermediation?

### 1.3 Census Reconstruction, Accounting Boundaries, and Roadmap

Answering these questions across a 33-year modern span draws on HKEX records, Bloomberg data, offering documents, and allotment announcements, with checks for the known weaknesses of each source. Standard vendor downloads commingle non-capital-raising introductions, GEM-to-Main-Board transfers, investment vehicles, and SPACs with genuine operating-company IPOs, while recycled stock codes, unadjusted share consolidations, missing delisting exits, and blank fee schedules systematically distort historical return and cost series (Appendix A details our full diagnostic audit and reconciliation waterfall).

## Section 2. Why Hong Kong? The Foundations of a Cross-Border Equity Market

Why did Mainland companies and international investors meet in Hong Kong? The question is harder than it looks. A share has no maturity date and no specific collateral. The buyer hands over cash today in exchange for a residual claim on profits earned in another legal system, under managers the buyer cannot easily replace (La Porta et al., 1998; Stulz, 1999). In the early 1990s a Mainland enterprise had capital needs, but its currency was not convertible and its company law was unfinished. A pension fund in Boston or London had capital, but it needed audited accounts, a convertible currency, and a court it could trust.

Hong Kong closed that gap with three things that worked only together: money investors could take home, a legal wrapper they recognized, and people who could translate a Mainland factory into an investable prospectus. None of the three was built for this purpose. Each came out of an earlier local problem, which is why the history matters.

### 2.1 Money Investors Could Take Home

The first requirement is plain. A foreign fund will not buy if it fears that dividends or sale proceeds cannot leave. Hong Kong's answer was forged in the confidence crisis around the Sino-British talks. In September 1983 the Hong Kong dollar fell to HK\$9.60 against the U.S. dollar. On October 17 the government fixed it at HK\$7.80 under a currency-board arrangement, later refined into the HK\$7.75–7.85 band. The Basic Law, promulgated in 1990, then carried the commitment across the 1997 handover: Chapter V requires the currency to be fully backed and bars foreign-exchange controls.

The peg solved one problem and created a price for solving it. It removed convertibility and repatriation risk, so a company with renminbi revenue could raise hard currency and a global fund could get its money out. But a fixed rate with open capital flows means importing U.S. monetary policy (the Mundell-Fleming logic), and large IPO subscriptions would later show up directly in interbank rates (Section 7.3). Nor does a stable currency protect anyone from overstated earnings or tunneling. Monetary credibility was necessary. Corporate credibility had to come from elsewhere.

### 2.2 A Legal Wrapper That Separated Assets from Governance

Hong Kong's second achievement was to pull apart three things that domestic markets usually fuse: where a company operates, where it is incorporated, and where its shares are listed and policed. In the language of the cross-listing literature, a firm could "bond" itself to a stricter regime abroad (Coffee, 1999, 2002; Stulz, 1999), although skeptics rightly warn that enforcement against insiders across a border is often incomplete (Licht, 2003; Siegel, 2005).

Two legal corridors emerged. The **offshore corridor** let businesses in Hong Kong or the Mainland list through holding companies in Common Law jurisdictions. Bermuda led in the first decade, after Jardine Matheson's 1984 redomiciliation made the route familiar. The Cayman Islands then took over and became the default for private Mainland entrepreneurs, including firms using variable-interest structures such as Tencent. The **H-share corridor** kept the company legally in China but wrote investor protections into its charter under Chapter 19A: class rights, connected-transaction controls, international accounting, and arbitration. The first corridor moved the firm to the law. The second brought the law to the firm.

The second corridor has since changed. China repealed the 1994 Mandatory Provisions in March 2023 when it adopted the new overseas-listing regime, and Hong Kong's rules for Mainland issuers were revised afterward. Class-meeting clauses stay binding until an issuer amends its articles, but the H-share package described here is a historical design that is now being simplified.

Across both corridors, the prospectus was the common legal interface. Wherever the factories stood, directors and other responsible parties faced Hong Kong prospectus and securities law (Section 6.4).

### 2.3 People Next Door, and the Luck of Position

Law and currency still need people. Allen, Qian, and Qian (2005) emphasize that what matters is whether working institutions connect informal commercial reality to formal rules. Hong Kong had banks, law firms, reporting accountants, valuers, printers, and regulators clustered within a few blocks. It also had what might be called dual proximity: close to Mainland factories and officials in language and geography, close to London and New York in legal habits and fiduciary standards.

We should not credit all of this to local virtue. The rule of law, simple taxes, and professional skills were genuine achievements. But the scale of Hong Kong's role also depended on a closed Mainland capital account, slow and administratively rationed A-share listing (Qian, Ritter, and Shao, 2024), and Beijing's decision to use Hong Kong as its main external equity window. Part of what Hong Kong earned was institutional return. Part was position. The rest of this monograph tries to keep those two apart, and Section 12 returns to how far the evidence allows it.

## Section 3. From Trading Port to Financial Infrastructure, 1891–1993

The prehistory answers one question: **when Mainland enterprises finally came looking for foreign capital in 1993, why was Hong Kong ready to receive them?** It was not ready because the market had grown smoothly. For most of its first century the exchange served a narrow clientele, played little part in financing local industry, and failed spectacularly more than once. The readiness came from those failures. The 1973 crash brought securities law. The 1987 closure brought an independent regulator. Settlement backlogs brought central clearing. By 1993 a speculative local market had been rebuilt, crisis by crisis, into something an international investor could use.

### 3.1 A Club That Did Not Finance the Factories (1891–1968)

Formal share trading began in February 1891, when mostly expatriate brokers formed the Association of Stockbrokers in Hong Kong, renamed the Hong Kong Stock Exchange in 1914. A rival exchange appeared in 1921. Trading stopped under Japanese occupation, and the two bodies merged in 1947. What came out of the war was an orderly but clubby, English-speaking market centered on trading houses, utilities, banks, and property.

Then came one of the most striking episodes in the city's economic history. After 1949, refugee industrialists, many of them Shanghai textile families, turned Hong Kong into an export manufacturer. And the stock exchange had almost nothing to do with it. Factories were financed by retained family profit and, above all, by bank credit, with HSBC at the center.

Why banks and not the bourse? Export orders turned over quickly against letters of credit, which made them natural bank collateral. Family owners valued secrecy and control and saw little reason to accept outside shareholders while their overdrafts kept rolling. And the exchange itself, with its English-speaking membership and high entry costs, was not built for them. Long-lived assets such as power plants and docks went to the equity market; working capital went to the banks. This is the pattern finance-and-growth theory would predict (Rajan and Zingales, 1998; Levine, 2005). It also sets up the rest of the story: on the eve of the 1970s, Hong Kong had a rich economy and a stock market that most of its people did not use.

### 3.2 Democratization, Mania, and the First Lesson in Regulation (1969–1986)

Two decades of industrial growth had put savings in Chinese hands, and a new generation of property developers wanted permanent capital. The incumbent exchange served neither group well. Ronald Li saw the opening and launched the Far East Exchange in December 1969, trading in Cantonese and welcoming local firms. The Kam Ngan and Kowloon exchanges followed. Share ownership became a popular pursuit, and the listings of 1972–1973, led by Cheung Kong and its peers, gave Chinese developers the currency to compete with the British *hongs*. Within a decade Cheung Kong would control Hutchison Whampoa and Y.K. Pao would control Wheelock. The local market had shifted the balance of corporate power in the city.

It also showed what competition among lightly supervised exchanges can do. Four broker-owned venues competed for listings, margin credit was loose, and there was no independent securities regulator. The Hang Seng Index rose to a peak of 1,775 in March 1973. Forged share certificates surfaced, credit tightened, the oil shock arrived, and by December 1974 the index stood near 150, a fall of more than 90 percent.

The damage produced the first lasting institutional response. The Securities Ordinance and the Protection of Investors Ordinance of 1974 created a formal supervisory framework, and unification legislation pushed the four exchanges toward one. In April 1986 they merged into the Stock Exchange of Hong Kong. The lesson was simple and it would be relearned on GEM decades later: when listing venues compete without a credible gatekeeper, standards fall first and investors pay later.

### 3.3 1987 and the Rebuilding That Made H-Shares Possible

One exchange did not mean good governance. Two episodes in 1987 tested the new institution, and both shaped what foreign investors would later be promised.

The first concerned voting rights. In March 1987, Cheung Kong, Hutchison, and Jardine Matheson proposed "B shares" that would give controlling holders votes at a fraction of the economic cost. Investors objected, prices fell, and regulators stepped in. The principle of proportional voting was then written into the listing rules. It protected minority investors for three decades and later cost Hong Kong the Alibaba IPO (Section 4.3).

The second was a crisis of confidence in the institution itself. After Wall Street crashed on October 19, the leveraged futures market threatened to bring down its clearing guarantee. The exchange, under Ronald Li, closed the stock and futures markets for four trading days. When trading resumed the Hang Seng fell by a third in one session, and a rescue package of about HK\$4 billion was needed. The Hay Davison report of 1988 described an exchange run as an inside club. Its remedy was structural: the Securities and Futures Commission was created in 1989 as an independent statutory regulator, exchange governance was opened up, and risk management was overhauled.

The plumbing followed. Settlement still depended on moving paper certificates between brokers and registrars, and volume spikes caused backlogs. The Central Clearing and Settlement System began in June 1992, and automatic order matching arrived in November 1993. A market that had nearly failed in 1987 could now process large international offerings.

This is the point at which the local story met the national one. After Deng Xiaoping's 1992 Southern Tour, Mainland leaders wanted foreign capital and modern corporate practice for state enterprises without giving up legal domicile. Vice Premier Zhu Rongji backed the effort, and Liu Hongru led the Mainland side in work with a Hong Kong team that included Charles Lee, Herbert Hui, and Laura Cha (Cha, 2023). The obstacle was large: China did not yet have a national Company Law, which was adopted only in December 1993. So the negotiators built a bridge by contract. A memorandum of regulatory cooperation was signed in June 1993, the Hong Kong listing rules gained Chapter 19A, and issuer charters carried the required protections, later standardized in the Mandatory Provisions of August 1994. To qualify, a state enterprise had to separate its productive business from schools, clinics, and housing, restate its accounts to international standards, and seat independent directors.

On July 15, 1993, Tsingtao Brewery began trading as the first H-share. Each ingredient had a history. The peg came from the 1983 run. The regulator came from the 1987 closure. Clearing came from years of settlement strain. Proportional voting came from the B-share fight. **Hong Kong was ready in 1993 largely because it had already paid for its mistakes.**

## Section 4. Five Modern Eras—and the Listings That Did Not Happen, 1994–2026

How did the bridge built in 1993 change over the next three decades? The short answer is that it was rebuilt about every ten years, usually after a crisis or after losing business to a rival venue. Table 4.1 gives the whole arc in numbers. Five eras stand out, and each has a different central character: state enterprises testing the market; state banks and the first private champions; global fundraising with widening cracks; new-economy listing reform; and an A+H industrial wave under geopolitical pressure.

### 4.1 Era I (1994–1999): H-Share Pioneers, Red Chips, and the First Great Test

The first wave brought provincial infrastructure, power, airlines, and heavy industry to international investors, followed by "Red Chips": Hong Kong- or Bermuda-incorporated groups controlled by Mainland ministries and municipalities. Enthusiasm peaked before the 1997 handover. Beijing Enterprises, listed in May 1997, raised about HK\$2.15 billion with a subscription ratio of 1,276 times (Lee and Poon, 2004), and China Telecom (Hong Kong), later China Mobile, raised over HK\$30 billion in October 1997.

The Asian Financial Crisis then tested every pillar built in the preceding decade. Speculators attacked the currency and the stock market together, Peregrine collapsed, and new listings dried up. The government bought about HK\$118 billion of shares in August 1998, later returning them to the market through the Tracker Fund. Two rule changes came out of this period. Practice Note 18 in 1998 set mandatory retail clawbacks, and the Growth Enterprise Market opened in 1999 as a disclosure-based board for younger firms. The first would shape allocation for a quarter-century. The second would become a lesson in what weak gatekeeping produces.

GEM shows how much the design relied on investors to protect themselves. Its 1999 rules set no profit test and asked for only a two-year business history. In exchange, applicants had to give prominent risk warnings and investors had to sign a risk acknowledgement. The Exchange summed up the principle as "let the buyer beware" (Stock Exchange of Hong Kong, 1999). If disclosure alone could discipline a young market, GEM was the place to see it. If it could not, the burden would fall on sponsors and on the regulator's later ability to step in. Section 4.4 records where the experiment led: in 2018 the easy path from GEM to the Main Board was closed.

### 4.2 Era II (2000–2007): The Exchange Becomes a Company and the State Banks Arrive

The era began with a change in the market operator itself. The exchange, futures exchange, and clearing house were merged into Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing, which listed its own shares in June 2000. A profit-seeking exchange had stronger reasons to pursue large listings and new products. It also had a lasting conflict between commercial growth and frontline regulation, which the 2003 dual-filing arrangement partly addressed by giving the SFC direct powers over listing disclosure.

The penny-stock episode of July 2002 exposed that conflict in public. On 25 July, HKEX proposed that shares quoted below HK\$0.50 be consolidated, with delisting possible if they were not. The next day, 577 of 761 Main Board stocks fell, and stocks at or below that price lost about HK\$10.9 billion, or about 10 percent of their market value. HKEX withdrew the continuing-listing proposals within days. The government's inquiry found that the Listing Committee, the designated gatekeeper, had been brought in only about two weeks before release, and it pointed to an occasionally tense relationship between HKEX and the SFC (Panel of Inquiry, 2002). Who should police a market when the policeman is also a listed business? Dual filing was one answer. It left the Exchange as the front line and gave the SFC its own statutory reach over listing disclosure from April 2003 (Securities and Futures Commission, n.d.).

China's entry into the WTO then opened the largest chapter of the story. Telecom, energy, insurance, and finally the state banks came to market. China Construction Bank, Bank of China, and ICBC raised sums that no earlier Asian market could have absorbed. These deals helped establish the cornerstone tranche, in which named investors commit early and accept a six-month lockup (Section 6.3). At the same time, a different kind of issuer appeared. BYD listed in 2002, and Tencent raised less than HK\$2 billion in 2004. Nobody at the time could have known that this small internet offering would become the largest single source of investor gains in the market's history (Section 8.5).

### 4.3 Era III (2008–2017): The Fundraising Crown and Three Cracks

After a sharp fall in 2008, Hong Kong had its largest decade by funds raised. AIA and Agricultural Bank of China anchored 2010. HKEX also courted global brands, from Glencore to Prada (Section 11.3). Stock Connect opened in 2014 and 2016, letting Mainland investors buy eligible Hong Kong shares through a closed loop. In 2017 the physical trading hall closed, a quiet marker of how far the market had moved from its origins.

Yet success hid three problems. First, accounting fraud reached the IPO gate: the Hontex case exposed false prospectus numbers and weak sponsor diligence, and the SFC's sponsor regime followed in 2013 (Section 6.4). Second, GEM became a shell factory. Many listings were small placings to narrow groups, first-day gains could be extraordinary, and the later collapse was severe. Lowering the entry gate in a market with limited private litigation and little small-cap short selling did not produce a venture market. Third, the one-share-one-vote rule met the internet economy. Alibaba's partnership structure was unacceptable in 2013, and its record 2014 IPO went to New York. Hong Kong had protected a principle and lost the most valuable Chinese company of the moment.

### 4.4 Era IV (2018–2022): The New-Economy Overhaul and the Reversal

In 2018 the exchange and regulator made their most ambitious changes since H-shares. GEM's easy path to the Main Board was closed and minimum listing sizes rose. New chapters then opened the door to companies the old rules had kept out: weighted-voting-rights firms (Chapter 8A), pre-revenue biotechs (Chapter 18A), and overseas-listed Greater China issuers seeking a secondary home (Chapter 19C). Xiaomi, Meituan, Innovent, BeiGene, and Alibaba's 2019 homecoming followed. Audit-access disputes and the U.S. Holding Foreign Companies Accountable Act of 2020 later made the secondary route more valuable than its designers had needed it to be.

The boom did not last. Beijing's 2021 campaigns in platforms, tutoring, and property met rising global interest rates. By 2021 close to half of Main Board IPOs closed below offer on the first day, and fundraising fell sharply in 2022. The era shows that a market can repair its rules and still be hit by politics and the rate cycle.

### 4.5 Era V (2023–2026Q3): Geopolitical Rewiring and the A+H Wave

Fundraising bottomed in 2023 at about HK\$46 billion, then rose to about HK\$88 billion in 2024, HK\$287 billion in 2025, and HK\$387 billion in the first nine months of 2026. Five changes lie behind the turn. Chapter 18C opened the Main Board to specialist technology companies. China's overseas-listing filing regime replaced case-by-case approval with a common procedure. Tighter A-share issuance after August 2023, combined with official support for Hong Kong listings by Mainland leaders in April 2024, redirected industrial champions southward. FINI shortened settlement and reduced the cash tied up in subscriptions. And the August 2025 allocation reform let issuers avoid large retail clawbacks (Section 7.2).

The result was a change in who lists. PRC-incorporated H-share issuers rose from about one in seven Main Board deals in Era IV to about two in three in Era V. Many were already listed in Shanghai or Shenzhen. CATL, Midea, Zijin Gold, Luxshare, and Zhongji Innolight were not young firms discovering public markets. They were established companies using Hong Kong to raise convertible capital and build international shareholder bases. The distinction matters throughout the paper: for an A+H issuer, the Hong Kong offering is an IPO on this exchange but not the company's first sale of public equity.

### 4.6 The Parallel Ledger: The Listings That Did Not Happen

A history of completed IPOs sees only the companies that reached the bell. The deals that stopped, left, or never arrived are just as informative about what the market could and could not do (Table 4.2).

Some never reached investors, because applications lapse or are withdrawn when markets tighten or regulators ask harder questions. Some were stopped at the last moment. Ant Group is the defining case: its dual listing was halted in November 2020, days before trading, after Mainland regulators intervened. Hong Kong's systems returned a huge volume of subscription money without disorder, but the episode made clear that Mainland supervisory authority can override an offshore listing timetable. Others went elsewhere. For two decades, China's internet leaders chose New York because Hong Kong would not accept their control structures or early losses. Some came and then faded: Glencore's Hong Kong register shrank to almost nothing, and Coach withdrew after trading thinned. Finally, some rules attracted little business, including Chapter 18B SPACs and the attempt to revive GEM.

### 4.7 Do Hong Kong IPOs Rise When Mainland Gates Close?

Common wisdom says yes. The data say: not automatically. Over 393 months, Hong Kong issuance shows familiar hot and cold waves, rising after strong markets and falling when liquidity tightens (Lowry and Schwert, 2002; Pástor and Veronesi, 2005). But during the historical A-share IPO freezes, Hong Kong averaged about five deals a month, compared with about eight in other months before mid-2021. The freezes often came with weak markets, and redirecting a listing across the border took far longer than a freeze lasted. These are simple averages rather than causal estimates, but they rule out the idea of an immediate substitution valve.

The picture changes after April 2024, when three conditions finally held together: onshore issuance had tightened, the overseas filing route was standardized, and policymakers openly supported Hong Kong listings by A-share leaders. Monthly issuance rose to nearly ten deals. H-share issuers, about one deal in six before that date, accounted for roughly seven deals in ten and four-fifths of funds raised. So the valve exists, but it opens only when the Mainland's restriction, a legal route, and a pool of ready issuers coincide.

**What this history can and cannot say.** The era narrative is a description of sequence. It shows that major rule changes often followed crises or lost business, and that issuer composition moved with policy. It does not isolate the effect of any one reform, because reforms arrived together with market cycles and shifts in the Mainland pipeline.


# PART II — HOW THE MARKET WORKS

Before opening the machinery, it helps to have the basic facts in view. Most Hong Kong IPOs sell new shares, so the money usually goes to the company. Going public is cheap for very large issuers and expensive for small ones. Mainland banks now sit on almost every sponsor team. Offer prices cannot go above the published range, and the average first-day return is positive even though about one deal in three closes below offer. After the first day, the average investor does slightly worse than the market, the typical stock does much worse, and a few extraordinary winners account for most of the gains.

The four sections that follow take those facts in the order money moves: from the issuer (Section 5), through the intermediaries (Section 6), into the pricing and allocation of shares (Section 7), and out into the aftermarket (Section 8). Each ends by separating what holds across the long sample from what depends on recent disclosure or an unusually hot market.

## Section 5. Issuers: Who Lists, Who Gets the Money, and What It Costs

**The facts first.** Are Hong Kong IPOs mainly a way for insiders to cash out? Generally not. Among offerings whose share structure can be verified, more than four in five sell only new shares, and about 94 cents of each dollar goes to the company. Is listing cheap? It depends on size. The largest issuers pay about 3 percent of proceeds in direct costs, and the smallest pay about 18 percent. And is the IPO the end of the financing story? For many listed firms it is closer to the beginning.

### 5.1 Who Receives the Money?

A common suspicion about offshore listings is that they mainly provide exits for founders and early investors. The evidence points the other way. Offer structures can be verified for 1,862 of the 2,866 IPOs (1,494 on the Main Board and 368 on GEM), and in that sample primary shares dominate. Sales by existing holders were more common from 2000 to 2017, but much of that reflected a Chinese policy requiring overseas-listed state firms to transfer shares equal to 10 percent of proceeds for the national social security fund. Once that mechanism changed in 2017 and scrutiny of founder sales increased, secondary selling almost vanished. In Era V nearly every Main Board IPO is purely primary.

What do companies say they will do with the money? In 192 recent prospectuses with itemized plans, the common answers are capacity, equipment, research, overseas supply chains, and working capital. Only 16 issuers plan to repay any debt. Those few draw weaker demand and have lower early returns, which fits the view that use-of-proceeds disclosure carries information (Leone, Rock, and Willenborg, 2007). With so few cases, we treat this as a suggestive pattern. There is also little sign that bank-affiliated sponsors steer IPO money toward repaying loans (Puri, 1996; Drucker and Puri, 2005).

These numbers tell us that cash entered the company and what management promised to do with it. They do not tell us whether it was spent well. Section 10.2 follows that question through individual firms.

Biotech and specialist-technology issuers are admitted on the strength of research or technology rather than an established profit record, and they rely more heavily on specialist investors.

### 5.3 What Does Going Public Cost?

Judged by underwriting fees alone, Hong Kong looks cheap. It has none of the U.S. clustering at 7 percent (Chen and Ritter, 2000; Abrahamson, Jenkinson, and Jones, 2011). Commissions are about 2 percent of proceeds on a dollar-weighted basis, and they fall with deal size.

Look at the whole bill and the answer changes (Ritter, 1987). Sponsors, lawyers in several jurisdictions, reporting accountants, valuers, printers, and regulators must be paid whether a company raises HK\$300 million or HK\$30 billion. In the smallest quartile, these non-underwriting costs are several times the commission. Total direct costs reach about 18 percent of proceeds, against about 3 percent in the largest quartile.

### 5.4 What Do the Pre-IPO Accounts Reveal?

Prospectus accounts give investors a last audited look before the market takes over. Our rule follows the Accountants' Report: Year-1 is its latest audited period, full-year or shorter, and Years-2 and -3 are the two complete financial years before it. Only sales, pre-tax profit, and net profit are annualized for stub periods. Three patterns stand out in the 2025–2026 sample.

First, investors appear to pay little attention to accrual quality on day one. Issuers with high and low accruals have almost identical first-day returns, but low-accrual firms subsequently do better (Section 8.1; Teoh, Welch, and Wong, 1998). The gap in cash conversion between these groups is large, though part of it follows mechanically from sorting on profit minus operating cash flow.

Second, about one R&D-active issuer in seven capitalizes development spending under IAS 38. These firms have grown more slowly and have lower first-day returns than those expensing all R&D.

Third, a group of issuers sits fairly close to the higher profit requirement introduced in 2022, and they grow more slowly than comfortably profitable peers. This resembles threshold management, as Chen, Lee, and Li (2008) find in an earlier Chinese setting. But our window extends well above the threshold and we have not run a density test, so we describe these firms as thin-margin issuers rather than claim bunching.

Have the companies coming to market changed? Their audited accounts suggest that they have. Before 2013, almost every Main Board issuer with usable accounts was profitable in its last audited period. Among 2021–2024 listings, about one in three reported a loss (Table 5.3). The timing fits the opening of the biotech, weighted-voting, and specialist-technology routes described in Section 5.2, which admitted firms that the old profit test would have kept out. Reported losses need careful reading, however. Pre-revenue science companies lose money by design, and accounting charges on investor shares can also produce a loss in a growing business.

Scale and leverage tell a different story. The typical issuer became smaller in the 2010s, when many small companies listed, and larger again after 2021, when minimum listing sizes had risen. Liabilities remained close to 60 percent of assets throughout. Going public has not meant arriving with an unusually clean balance sheet.

## Section 6. Intermediaries and Enforcement: Who Vouches for the Deal?

**The facts first.** Three decades ago, Hong Kong and British houses led most Main Board IPOs. Today Mainland banks lead about three in four and appear on the sponsor team in about nine in ten. Bookrunner syndicates have grown from about one bank to about eight. Cornerstone investors appeared in about two in five Main Board deals in 2008–2017 and now appear in three of four. The central question is what these changes do to trust: who vouches for an offering, and what happens if they are wrong?

### 6.1 Four Different Jobs

A Hong Kong IPO divides work that a U.S. reader may expect to see combined. **Sponsors** are the gatekeepers, responsible for due diligence and for dealing with the regulator. **Overall coordinators and bookrunners** run the institutional sale and advise on price and allocation. Since August 2022, a sponsor-affiliated overall coordinator must generally be appointed at least two months before the listing application. **Receiving banks and retail brokers** bring in public applications and often provide financing. **Reporting accountants and valuers** verify the numbers and assets. Major international accounting firms and their predecessors worked on more than four-fifths of all IPOs in the census.

### 6.2 From British Houses to Mainland Banks

Who leads Hong Kong's IPOs has changed as dramatically as who issues them. Sorting Main Board lead roles into three groups gives a clear three-act story (Table 6.1). Local and British-linked houses dominated the 1990s. Global banks rose with the large privatizations of the 2000s and peaked in Era III. Mainland institutions then took over as their clients, capital, and Hong Kong platforms grew. Since underwriter reputation is one way an issuer borrows credibility (Beatty and Ritter, 1986; Carter and Manaster, 1990), this handover also changed whose name stands behind a deal.

### 6.3 Cornerstone Investors: Certification or Selection?

What do cornerstones actually do? They agree before the public offer to buy a fixed amount at the final price, are named in the prospectus, and accept a six-month lockup. The practice grew out of the giant state-bank offerings and was formalized in exchange guidance (McGuinness, 2014). In Era V a typical deal with cornerstones places around two-fifths of its base offering with them.

The 2025–2026 data suggest three roles. The first is **certification**. A respected investor's commitment may tell others that the deal has been examined. Biotech and technology issuers allowed to bring existing investors in as cornerstones have strong first-day results, in line with the view that informed insiders can add credibility, although only seven deals fall in this group. The second is **assured distribution**. As Rule 144A participation receded, Mainland state-linked and local-government funds took a larger place in the book. The third is an unintended one: **float restriction**. Shares placed with cornerstones cannot trade for six months, which reduces the supply available to everyone else (Section 8.3).

### 6.4 What Makes the Promise Credible?

Cross-border investing works only if a false prospectus has consequences even when assets sit across a border (Coffee, 2002; Siegel, 2005). Hong Kong's response developed in steps. The 2003 dual-filing arrangement gave the SFC a direct role in reviewing listing disclosure. *Hontex* tested enforcement: a Mainland fabric company that listed in 2009 was found to have materially overstated its prospectus figures, and in 2012 the SFC secured a court order requiring about HK\$1 billion to be returned to public investors. Its sponsor, Mega Capital, was fined and lost its license for the relevant business.

The case led directly to reform. From October 2013, Paragraph 17 of the SFC Code of Conduct set out detailed sponsor duties, and draft Application Proofs became public early in the process. Paragraph 17 is a licensing standard enforced through regulatory sanctions. Prospectus liability itself rests in the general law, and the SFC's 2012 proposals specifically asked how that law should apply to sponsors. The practical effect was to make diligence more demanding and failure more costly. In 2018 the exchange added another tool by allowing long-suspended companies to be delisted, reducing the number of shells left in limbo.

This closes a loop with Section 5.3. Better gatekeeping is part of what makes the market acceptable to global investors. It is also part of the fixed cost that weighs most heavily on small issuers.

**Evidence boundary.** The change in intermediary leadership is a 33-year fact. The classification by bank origin involves judgment because Mainland-owned banks operate through Hong Kong entities and global banks may employ Mainland-focused teams. The cornerstone taxonomy is rich but covers only 21 months, and six-month outcomes exist for the 161 issuers that had reached that horizon by the cutoff. It supports description of who invests where. It does not identify a causal cornerstone effect.

## Section 7. Pricing and Allocation: Who Gets the First-Day Gain?

**The facts first.** Hong Kong IPOs have historically gained about 14 percent on the first day on average, while the median deal gains only about 2.5 percent and about one in three falls. Not one of nearly two thousand offerings with a price range priced above that range. Over the full period, the difference between first-day close and offer price amounts to more than HK\$600 billion left on the table. Who receives it depends on how shares are allocated, how applications are financed, and how far demand exceeds supply.

### 7.1 Why the Top of the Range Binds

In a U.S. bookbuild, strong demand can push the price above the filing range, rewarding investors who reveal information (Benveniste and Spindt, 1989; Hanley, 1993; Cornelli and Goldreich, 2001). In Hong Kong the public offer runs alongside the institutional book, and retail investors apply on the basis of a published maximum price. That maximum therefore works as a ceiling. Across all 1,943 HKEX deals with a classified range (1,702 Main Board and 241 GEM), none priced above it. A small number priced below the range, and they performed poorly.

The ceiling creates an asymmetry. When the market rises during the offer period, underwriters can adjust only up to the cap, and further good news does not show up in first-day returns in a measurable way. When the market falls, issuers are reluctant to go below the bottom of the range, so bad news passes into first-day returns. Price discovery is therefore constrained on both sides, but in different ways.

Recent deals have split into two pricing styles. Some domestic growth issuers use a single fixed price, and they often draw very heavy retail demand and high first-day returns. A+H issuers often state only a maximum price tied to the A-share market. The first style leaves more to the aftermarket to discover. The second leans on a price the onshore market has already set.

### 7.2 Retail Clawback and the 2025 Reform

For 27 years, Practice Note 18 linked retail demand to retail supply. If the public tranche was sufficiently oversubscribed, shares moved automatically from institutions to the public (Agarwal, Liu, and Rhee, 2008; Vong and Trigueiros, 2017). The rule offers something close to a natural experiment. Deals just above the 15-times trigger receive a much larger retail allocation than deals just below it, and they have lower first-day returns. More supply at the margin appears to absorb some excess demand. At very high demand, both the price cap and the clawback cap bind, and extra enthusiasm has nowhere to go but the aftermarket (Derrien, 2005; Ljungqvist, Nanda, and Singh, 2006).

The August 2025 reform gave issuers a choice. Mechanism A starts with a small public tranche and retains a clawback. Mechanism B fixes the public tranche, with no clawback. Issuers answered clearly: more than nine in ten eligible deals chose Mechanism B, usually near its 10 percent minimum.

Did this improve the market? The answer depends on the objective. Retail allocations and first-day flipping fell, and institutions and cornerstones gained a larger share of the first-day gain. But first-day returns were also high in a booming market, and the reform is hard to separate from that boom. Oversubscription itself is measured after the rule change and can be affected by it, so comparisons between high-demand and other deals should be treated as descriptive. The defensible conclusion is that the reform changed who receives the shares. Its effect on pricing is not yet clear.

### 7.3 Financing the Queue: Margin, HIBOR, and FINI

Retail participation in Hong Kong is cheap to enter and easy to lever. A single board lot in 2026 typically costs a few thousand Hong Kong dollars, and brokers offer margin financing for larger applications. Early margin demand predicts final subscription, consistent with investors watching one another (Welch, 1992).

For many years this had a visible money-market side effect. Applications required large amounts of cash to be held during the offer, and the biggest deals could tighten short-term funding. FINI, introduced in November 2023, shortened the time between pricing and trading and replaced much of the prefunding with a more targeted confirmation process. The average interval from prospectus to listing fell from about two weeks to about nine days, and from pricing to listing from about seven days to about four.

Did FINI remove the funding pressure? The pattern is favorable: short-term rates behaved more calmly around very large post-FINI subscriptions. But few large deals took place just before the reform, and the change coincided with other monetary conditions. We read this as evidence that FINI eased the pressure, while recognizing that a clean before-and-after comparison is unavailable.

### 7.4 Do Retail Investors Really Earn the First-Day Return?

The headline number says yes. In 2026, the average IPO rose by more than 50 percent on its first day. The allotment tables say no. An investor is not paid on the shares requested, but on the shares received, and the hot deals are the ones in which an applicant gets little or nothing. This is the logic of Rock (1986), tested in an earlier setting by Koh and Walter (1989).

### 7.5 The First Day and the First Month

Does the opening auction give a fair first price? On average, yes. Across more than two thousand Main Board deals the opening return and closing return are nearly the same, and the opening price is close to an unbiased forecast of the close (Biais, Hillion, and Spatt, 1999).

After that, stabilization comes into play. For about 30 days, a stabilizing manager may support the shares at or below the offer price and use the over-allotment arrangement (Aggarwal, 2000). The return distribution shows a noticeable concentration at exactly the offer price and more deals just above it than just below. Part of that pattern reflects the mass at zero and price tick sizes, so we treat it as consistent with price support and do not attach a precise size to it. Some weak deals also lose ground once support ends, and non-A+H issues see turnover fall when the stabilization period expires.

**Evidence boundary.** The price ceiling, the clawback discontinuity, and the opening-auction result rest on long samples and are structural. The ballot evidence is exact for 2026 only, and the very high returns of that year are cyclical. Mechanism B and FINI have short histories, and their effects are entangled with a strong market.

## Section 8. After the IPO: Who Actually Makes Money?

**The facts first.** Do Hong Kong IPOs underperform? The answer depends on when you buy, how you weight, and which period you examine. Bought at the first-day close and held for a year, the average Main Board IPO trails a dividend-inclusive market index by about 4 percent, and the typical IPO trails it by about 20 percent. Bought at the offer price, the average IPO beats that index by about 5 percent. And weighted by proceeds, most of the shortfall disappears. Over the very long run, about three-quarters of verified IPOs sit below their offer price, while a very small number of firms generate most of the gains.

Three conditional answers emerge.

*Does the IPO investor underperform?* If entry is at the first-day close, yes on average. If entry is at the offer price, no. The difference is the first-day gain. But the offer-price result describes a hypothetical investor who receives every deal in proportion. Actual allocations are not observed, and Section 7.4 shows how much rationing can change the answer.

*Is this a general feature of the 33-year sample?* Much less than the pooled figure suggests. Cohorts from 1994 through 2017 are, on average, about level with the dividend-inclusive index. The negative pooled figure comes mainly from 2018–2022 and especially from the recent cohorts, whose one-year windows include very strong market conditions for established stocks and sharp reversals for hot issues. Median underperformance, by contrast, appears in every era.

*Does it survive risk adjustment?* Only in part. In calendar-time portfolios, equal-weighted Main Board IPOs show negative alphas in the weighted three-factor specification, while value-weighted portfolios are not reliably different from zero. Adding a book-to-market factor weakens the equal-weighted result further. GEM stays deeply negative in every specification. These portfolios begin after listing, so they speak to secondary-market holders, not to allocations at the offer price. The most consistent reading is that underperformance is concentrated in smaller, equal-weighted Main Board exposure and in GEM (Fama and French, 1993; Lyon, Barber, and Tsai, 1999).

Prospectus information also lines up with subsequent returns in the recent sample. Low-accrual firms outperform high-accrual firms over six months. R&D expensers do better than capitalizers. Issuers that plan to repay debt do poorly. Higher first-day returns are followed by weaker one-year performance. These are useful signals, but they rest on a short and unusual window, and the six-month returns for recent issuers are raw buy-and-hold returns.

### 8.2 Lockups: Why Legal Form Changes the Supply Calendar

Lockup expiry adds shares at a known date, yet prices often weaken around it (Field and Hanka, 2001; Brav and Gompers, 2003). Hong Kong gives an unusual comparison. In many offshore-incorporated issuers, cornerstone and pre-IPO investor shares can come free around six months. In PRC-incorporated issuers, Company Law freezes pre-IPO shares for a year, so only the cornerstone shares are released at six months.

The estimated drop is large for a scheduled event, however, and the Cayman group is small and concentrated in a volatile period, so the size should be treated with care until more cohorts mature.

### 8.3 Where Does the Liquidity Go?

First-day trading is intense and rarely lasts. In most eras, a large majority of Main Board IPOs lose more than 95 percent of their first-day volume by Month 6, and many have days with no trades at all. GEM is worse. Larger offerings hold liquidity better on the Amihud (2002) price-impact measure. The participation of online retail brokers is associated with fewer zero-volume days, and heavy cornerstone lockups with weaker trading afterward. Since first-day volume is unusually high everywhere, a decline from that level is not itself a sign of failure. The informative result is the long tail of issues that end up barely traded.

### 8.4 Stock Connect: More Trading, Not One Market

Does Southbound Stock Connect fix illiquidity and close the gap between H- and A-share prices? The answer is yes for part of the first problem and no for the second (Jia, Wang, and Xiong, 2017).

![Figure 8.2: Post-IPO liquidity decay across Main Board eras and the persistent A+H valuation wedge before and after Stock Connect. Panel (b) traces mean H-share versus onshore A-share price discounts from IPO offer price through Day-1 close and post-stabilization automatic Southbound Connect admission.](figures/master_fig8_2_liquidity_and_ah_wedge.png)

Non-A+H IPOs admitted to Connect see very large increases in turnover relative to peers, but admission typically comes through index inclusion, and the companies that qualify are already different. A+H issues offer a cleaner test because they become eligible automatically after stabilization. Their turnover rises much more modestly, and Mainland investors come to hold a sizable part of the H-share float. The clean estimate is the better guide to what Connect itself contributes.

Prices tell a different story. In recent A+H deals, the H-share offer is priced at a discount of about one-third to the A-share. Trading narrows the gap somewhat on the first day, but Connect eligibility hardly changes it. A and H shares are not fungible, cross-market arbitrage is limited, and dividend taxes differ. Connect links order flow without merging the two markets.

### 8.5 The Big Reconciliation: Most IPOs Lose, the Total Is Large

We can now return to the puzzle in Section 1. How can about three-quarters of verified IPOs stand below offer while the sum of price changes on the offered shares is about HK\$3.7 trillion?

The answer is skewness, the mechanism Bessembinder (2018) documents for U.S. stocks. In our verified sample, the median IPO is down by more than 60 percent. But the top 1 percent, just 21 companies, account for nearly three-quarters of all positive gains. Tencent alone accounts for almost 30 percent of the net total.

# PART III — WHAT THE MARKET MADE POSSIBLE

Part II showed how the money moves. Part III asks what it left behind. Who benefited in Hong Kong itself? What became of the Mainland companies that came to market? Whose capital was involved, and what would have happened if the bridge had not existed?

## Section 9. What the IPO Market Built in Hong Kong

**The facts first.** IPO money mostly passes through Hong Kong. What stays is the work surrounding it: legal, accounting, and advisory services at the time of the deal, then years of trading, clearing, data, and follow-on business. For HKEX, trading and post-trade income far exceeds listing fees. For the public, the most memorable IPOs were often not ordinary corporate listings but public-asset sales such as the Tracker Fund, MTR, and Link.

### 9.1 What Counts as Hong Kong's Gain?

The first step is to avoid counting the wrong thing. IPO proceeds are not Hong Kong output. When global investors buy CATL shares to fund a plant in Hungary, the money passes through Hong Kong, but only the value added by resident producers enters local GDP. Fees count only to the extent that they are earned and produced here. Capital gains belong to shareholders wherever they live, and they add to Hong Kong household wealth only for local residents. Financial services as a whole account for roughly a quarter of GDP, but that sector is dominated by banking, insurance, and asset management, so it is context and not a measure of the IPO effect.

Figure 9.1 traces these boundaries for a dollar raised in Hong Kong.

### 9.2 Four Channels of Local Value

**Deal-time services.** In the 216 recent IPOs with complete schedules, listing expenses total about 4 percent of proceeds. Roughly half of those expenses go to non-underwriting services: lawyers, accountants, internal-control advisers, valuers, printers, registrars, and regulators. On small deals this share is close to four-fifths, and on the largest it is about one-third. We should be careful with the implication. Those payments are not all earned in Hong Kong, since Mainland and international counsel, industry consultants, and offshore offices are in the mix. Underwriting fees, conversely, may be partly earned by Hong Kong staff. Prospectuses show what kind of service was bought, but not where the value was produced. So "half the bill" is the amount exposed to local professional services, and Hong Kong's actual share remains to be measured.

![Figure 9.2: Non-underwriting fee shares across deal-size tiers and two decades of recurring HKEX Group revenue architecture. Panel (a) decomposes HK\$24.08 billion of disclosed listing expenses across 216 audited 2025Q1–2026Q3 Main Board IPOs. Panel (b) contrasts Stock Exchange listing fees, which include both initial and annual fees, with trading, clearing, custody, market-data, and investment income across FY2005–FY2025.](figures/master_fig9_2_fees_and_hkex_revenue.png)

**The recurring exchange business.** A listing can generate income for years. HKEX's listing fees, which combine initial charges with annual fees paid by listed companies, have fallen to about 6 percent of group revenue. Trading, clearing, depository, and market-data income was more than eleven times listing fees in 2025. This group total also includes derivatives and the London Metal Exchange, so it is not attributable to IPOs alone. Its message is about business model: the exchange earns most of its revenue after Day 1.

Public finance follows the same rhythm. Hong Kong taxes neither capital gains nor dividends, so government revenue from the stock market comes largely through stamp duty on transfers. Total stamp duty, which also includes property, rose to about HK\$100 billion in 2021–22, fell to about HK\$49 billion in 2023–24, and recovered to about HK\$64 billion in 2024–25. The fiscal link runs through secondary-market activity and not through the amount raised in IPOs.

**People and capabilities.** Thirty years of cross-border offerings also trained lawyers, accountants, compliance officers, and regulators who understand both Mainland business and Common Law expectations. These skills transfer to follow-on deals, restructurings, acquisitions, and wealth management. They are real, though harder to measure than fees.

**Who shares in it?** The benefits are uneven. High-paying financial and professional jobs are concentrated among a mobile elite. Small local firms face entry costs that can reach nearly a fifth of the money raised. Ordinary savers can earn small gains from one-lot applications (Section 7.4), but the median long-run IPO outcome is poor (Section 8.5).

### 9.3 Following a Dollar

Figure 9.1 can be read in three steps. First, nearly all of each dollar with a verified structure goes to the issuer as new capital, and a small part goes to selling shareholders. Second, a few cents go to fees at the time of listing: about four cents on a dollar-weighted basis, but far more for a small issuer. Third, most of the net proceeds move on to factories, research, or bank capital, usually outside Hong Kong. What remains is the listed security and the stream of business it can generate.

### 9.4 When the Public Became the Investor

Three public-asset listings show a different side of the market. Tracker Fund and Link REIT are outside our operating-company census. MTR is inside it, but it was a government share sale and raised no money for the railway.

The channels above are therefore well documented, but the size of Hong Kong's retained benefit remains an open measurement problem.

## Section 10. Financing China's Corporate Transformation

What did listing in Hong Kong change inside Mainland companies? For state enterprises in the 1990s, the most important change came before the first share was sold. For later firms the answer varies widely, from a recapitalized bank to a technology company that hardly needed the IPO cash.

### 10.1 Reorganizing the State Enterprise

In the early 1990s, a Chinese state factory could be a production unit, landlord, school operator, clinic, and pension provider all at once. To list in Hong Kong it had to become something outsiders could value. Commercial assets were carved into a joint-stock company, social functions stayed behind with the parent, accounts were restated, independent directors were appointed, and minority protections were added (Cha, 2023). Research on share-issue privatization finds improvements in transparency and performance, though continuing state control limited how far market discipline could go (Sun and Tong, 2003; Jiang, Yue, and Zhao, 2009).

The large banks need especially careful attribution. Did Hong Kong IPOs rescue China's banking system? Not by themselves (IMF, 2006; Bank of China, 2008). The sequence had five parts. The state injected capital. Asset management companies removed bad loans. Banks became joint-stock companies and brought in strategic investors. H-share offerings then supplied a market price, disclosure discipline, and hard-currency equity. Later rights issues gave them a way to replenish capital. The IPO was a late and important stage in a state-led repair.

**ICBC: capital for a state bank.** ICBC arrived after state recapitalization, bad-loan disposal, and strategic investment. Its 2006 offering strengthened capital and placed the bank under international scrutiny, and rights issues later showed the value of continuing market access. The IPO complemented sovereign restructuring and did not replace it.

**Tencent: legitimacy more than cash.** Tencent raised less than HK\$2 billion in 2004 and described its use of proceeds only in general terms. It has since funded its growth through operating cash flow and debt, without a public placing or rights issue. What the listing gave it was a recognized legal and market status, a currency for employee incentives, and access to global credit. The offered shares went on to produce the largest gain in the census.

**CATL: offshore money for an overseas plant.** CATL was already listed in Shenzhen and held a very large cash balance. So why come to Hong Kong? Its prospectus points to the Hungarian plant: most of the proceeds were earmarked for that project. For a company building abroad, convertible offshore funds and an international shareholder base were worth more than one more pool of renminbi.

**Innovent: staged financing for science.** A pre-revenue biotech cannot fund a decade of development in one offering. Innovent listed in 2018, returned to investors through placings, signed partnerships, and eventually reached commercial scale. Its success also depended on clinical results and reimbursement policy, and it is one of the survivors. Many biotech stories end less happily.

**Ascletis and the counterexamples.** Listing around the same time as Innovent, Ascletis saw its commercial plans falter and its shares fall sharply. Shinsun met the Mainland property downturn soon after listing. Razer used its Hong Kong listing to pursue a broader consumer ecosystem and was later taken private below its offer price. These cases are a useful reminder that an IPO prices a plan. It cannot guarantee that the plan will work.

**Evidence boundary.** Case studies show mechanisms. Without a matched group of comparable unlisted firms, they cannot estimate how much investment, innovation, or productivity was caused by listing. That remains the most important unanswered real-economy question in this field.

## Section 11. Global Capital: Who Was on the Other Side?

**The facts first.** Mainland banks now dominate the sell side of Hong Kong IPOs. It does not follow that Mainland investors supply all the demand. For most of the period, overseas investors made up around two-fifths of cash-market trading, and Hong Kong's asset-management industry still draws much of its money from abroad. But Southbound trading has grown rapidly, and repeat cornerstone investors now include many Mainland institutions. On the most direct question, who buys the IPO shares, available data remain incomplete.

### 11.1 Who Finances Hong Kong's IPOs?

![Figure 11.1: Demand-side investor origin, Southbound Connect turnover, and asset-management AUM versus primary syndicate Sinification, 2000–2025. Panel (a) disaggregates HKEX Cash Market Transaction Survey (CMTS) overseas investor turnover across 2000/01–2020, separating Southbound Connect from Rest of Asia and UK from Continental Europe in the final 2020 survey. Panel (b) contrasts Southbound Connect turnover scaling against SFC AWMAS non-HK investor AUM and Era V PRC sponsor participation.](figures/master_fig11_1_investor_origin_connect.png)

HKEX's cash-market surveys through 2020 show a stable total overseas share and a major change in its composition. In 2000/01, the U.K. and the U.S. supplied most overseas trading, and Mainland China hardly registered. By 2020 Mainland investors, through Connect, had become one of the largest sources, and Asia as a whole was about as important as Europe and the U.S. combined. After 2020, Southbound turnover grew about fivefold. Meanwhile Hong Kong's asset-management pool reached a record in 2025, with most of the money sourced from outside the city.

These facts rebut the simple claim that Mainland sponsors imply an exclusively Mainland investor base. But they do not measure IPO demand. Turnover is secondary-market activity, the surveys end in 2020, and assets managed in Hong Kong need not be invested in Hong Kong IPOs. The named cornerstone data offer the closest primary-market evidence, and the most active repeat investors include several Mainland insurers, fund managers, and bank wealth units alongside global names. A dollar-weighted classification of cornerstone investors by ultimate capital source is feasible and would improve on anything now available.

### 11.2 What Global Portfolios and Issuers Received

For international investors, Hong Kong solved a portfolio problem. For much of the period China's domestic market was hard to access, yet China was becoming a major part of world output. Hong Kong offered a convertible, Common Law route into that growth, and the sectors on offer moved with the economy: first infrastructure, energy, and finance; then internet platforms; then biotechnology; now advanced manufacturing and clean technology.

Does the poor median IPO return mean these investors were foolish? Not necessarily. Large investors generally hold bigger and more liquid firms, and value-weighted calendar-time returns are much closer to the market than equal-weighted ones. Still, access does not guarantee excess return (Section 8.5). The reasonable claim is that Hong Kong supplied exposure that was otherwise difficult to obtain.

Issuers gained something too: listed equity for acquisitions and staff compensation, international financial reporting that helps with overseas customers and partners, and easier entry to global credit markets. These are important motives for firms that do not need the IPO cash.

### 11.3 Why Global Brands Came, and Why Many Drifted Away

Around 2010, Hong Kong sought to become an Asian home for global companies. Glencore, Prada, Samsonite, L'Occitane, and others listed on the promise of proximity to Asian growth and investors. The census contains only 37 IPOs from outside Greater China and the usual offshore jurisdictions, but they reveal a clear limit.

Getting listed was the easy part. Keeping an active market was harder. Foreign-incorporated primary listings were long outside Southbound Connect, cutting them off from a growing source of liquidity. Glencore's Hong Kong register became tiny, and Coach withdrew after trading faded. Others took on a discount or sought listings closer to home. L'Occitane went private, Razer did as well, Samsonite pursued a U.S. listing, and Ferretti added Milan.

Standard Chartered is the instructive exception. It has remained relevant in Hong Kong because the city is central to its actual business. A brand story can win a listing. Sustained trading needs local investors, local earnings, or access to the Mainland's investor base.

**Evidence boundary.** Investor-origin evidence is strongest for secondary trading before 2020 and weakest for recent primary allocations. The international-issuer analysis is based on a small number of cases and should be read as institutional comparison.

## Section 12. Counterfactuals, Trade-offs, and Conclusion

What would have happened without Hong Kong? No dataset can answer that directly. But comparing what each alternative could offer at each stage helps identify what Hong Kong added and what it merely inherited from restrictions elsewhere.

### 12.1 Era-by-Era Counterfactuals

### Table 12.1 — Era-by-Era Counterfactual Matrix: Hong Kong vs. Contemporary Alternatives (1994–2026)

| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Eras I–II (1994–2007)** | SOE Pioneers & Mega-Banks (*Tsingtao*, *ICBC*, *BOC*, *Tencent*) | Illiquid, non-tradable state shares; administrative quotas & frequent IPO freezes (Qian, Ritter, & Shao, 2024) | Open to select SOEs (*China Life*, *PetroChina*), but **SOX 2002** & U.S. litigation raised entry barriers | London captured select resources; Singapore lacked mega-cap depth | **Hard-currency USD/HKD price discovery, IFRS governance surgery, and Cayman-VIE accommodation** when A-shares could not absorb mega-SOEs or private tech |

**Against the A-share market.** In the early decades, Shanghai and Shenzhen could not readily absorb very large offerings, offered limited tradable float, and operated under administrative control of timing and pricing. Later reforms narrowed the gap, especially for domestic technology firms. Yet money raised onshore remains in a controlled capital account. That is why the case for Hong Kong is now strongest for a firm like CATL that wants convertible funds for overseas investment. The issuance freezes themselves were not an immediate source of Hong Kong deals (Section 4.7). The channel worked through a longer process of company restructuring and policy change.

**Against New York.** The United States offered deep capital and tolerance for dual-class shares and losses, and for two decades it won China's internet leaders. But it also brought litigation risk, compliance costs, a distant trading day, and after 2020 serious political and audit-related uncertainty. Hong Kong gained partly by reforming its own rules in 2018 and partly because the American route became harder.

**Against London and Singapore.** Both have strong legal systems and financial expertise. Neither had Hong Kong's combination of Mainland proximity, the depth of Chinese issuer specialization, and above all Stock Connect.

**Against banks and private capital.** Banks can finance factories and working capital. They are poorly suited to fund long, uncertain research or to provide loss-absorbing equity to themselves. Venture and guidance funds can nurture companies, but they need a public market in which to exit and recycle capital.

Hong Kong's advantage was therefore a joint product. Some of it was built: a credible currency, legal institutions, regulators, clearing systems, and skilled people. Some came from its position beside a large economy with a closed capital account. We cannot give a precise split. What the history does show is that Hong Kong gained when it adapted and lost business when its rules became rigid.

### 12.2 Four Trade-offs That Remain

**Access for the largest firms, high barriers for small ones.** The same verification that gives investors confidence creates costs that are trivial for a mega-issuer and heavy for a small firm. With GEM largely dormant, Hong Kong serves large and late-stage companies better than local small enterprises.

**Credibility versus friction.** Stronger sponsor duties and enforcement protect the market's reputation. They also raise the fixed cost of entry. There is no setting of these rules that delivers maximum protection and minimum cost at once.

**Certainty at issue versus liquidity afterward.** Cornerstones and Mechanism B give issuers a more secure book and reduce first-day flipping. They also leave a thin initial float and concentrate future supply around lockup dates.

**A deep Mainland pipeline versus dependence on it.** Mainland firms have revived the market, and H-share issuers dominate recent activity. That leaves Hong Kong's calendar exposed to Mainland policy and industrial cycles, while efforts to retain international issuers remain unfinished.

### 12.3 Four Lessons

**Averages can be true and misleading at once.** A market can raise large amounts of productive capital, generate substantial aggregate gains, and still leave most individual IPOs below their offer price. The reconciliation lies in entry price, weighting, and skewness. Whether the aggregate beats a passive alternative depends on the benchmark and is driven by a few cohorts and companies.

**Cross-border trust is built, and it costs money.** Hong Kong did not attract capital by removing rules. It combined a credible currency with adaptable legal structures, international accounting, and enforcement. The cost falls most heavily on small firms.

**Connection is not integration.** Stock Connect changes who can trade and how much they trade, but it leaves A and H shares as separate claims in separate markets. Their price gap persists.

**A financial center keeps the relationship more than the money.** IPO proceeds soon leave for factories and laboratories. What stays is a set of skills, services, and recurring market activity. The size of that retained value is the question this monograph can frame but not yet fully measure.

### 12.4 What We Still Do Not Know

Five questions seem most valuable for future work. Who actually receives IPO allocations, and what do they earn after accounting for rationing and holding periods? How much of the fee bill is produced by Hong Kong residents? What do IPO firms raise after listing, firm by firm? Does listing change investment, innovation, and productivity when firms are compared with credible unlisted peers? And after a full market cycle, did Mechanism B and the A+H wave improve price discovery, or did they mainly coincide with a bull market? The history in these pages explains why those questions matter. Answering them will need data the market has only recently begun to produce.

### A.2 How the Data Were Built

The census is built from HKEX records and Fact Books, Bloomberg Equity Capital Markets data, market price histories, and offering documents and allotment results. Bloomberg supplies much of the historical deal and return information. Their coverage differs. Online filings were harvested for 2,408 IPOs, 229 listings fall before the online filing cutoff, and the 229 records in the 2025–2026 panels were built with field-level source review. Earlier filing variables come largely from machine-assisted extraction, so they should not be read as if each had been individually checked by hand. For this revision we rechecked three filing fields. Public-offer subscription multiples now come from allotment announcements wherever the text allows, usually confirmed by dividing shares applied for by shares offered. Cornerstone status comes from prospectus sections and allotment announcements, including the older term "corporate investor." Cornerstone tranche shares (`ext_cornerstone_pct`) across the 725 confirmed 1994–2024 cornerstone deals are similarly rebuilt from allotment share counts and explicit prospectus commitments normalized by initial pre-greenshoe offer shares (rather than post-greenshoe totals), yielding verified allocations for 718 of 725 issuers while leaving 7 unquantified or boilerplate-only cases blank. The clawback design uses the deals with checked final allocations. Table A.2 shows the coverage of the main fields.

Five data problems matter most. **Recycled stock codes** can splice unrelated companies together, as when Glencore's former code 0805 was later reassigned. **Reverse splits and consolidations** can create false returns when corporate actions are not adjusted. **Delisting** can produce survivorship bias (Shumway, 1997), and delisting does not always mean failure, since some firms are acquired or privatized. **Placeholder zeros** can understate fees. **Financial-statement extraction** requires care with units, signs, and short reporting periods. Year-1 is always the latest audited period in the Accountants' Report, and only sales, pre-tax profit, and net profit are annualized when that period is shorter than a year. A check for this revision found serious errors in the earlier machine-read 1994–2024 accounting fields, including calendar years captured as profit, losses recorded as gains, and unit errors. We discarded those fields. For 2005–2024 we rebuilt them from the consolidated or combined statements in each Accountants' Report. A value is kept only if pre-tax profit, tax, and net profit reconcile, or if assets equal liabilities plus equity, and if the reporting period, currency, and scale pass further checks. Anything else stays blank. A blind reread of 90 randomly chosen issuers, made before the last two screens were added, found errors in two, one in units and one from a subsidiary holding company's report; both screens target those errors, but these data have not been individually reviewed by hand. Accounting data before 2005 are not used. Apart from Table 5.3, the financial statements used in Sections 5.4 and 8.1 come from the separately checked 2025–2026 panel.
